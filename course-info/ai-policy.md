# AI policy

**TL;DR** The policy seeks to maximize your learning. You may use AI tools for things that in ordinary circumstances you could use a friend or classmate for (discussion, for example),

BUT I recommend you have discussions with people first, because it is more rewarding if sometimes less efficient

AND you may use it for some other defined tasks that are not central to the learning goals of this course, such as figure drawing and slide making.

**Motivation:** The goal of this policy is to maximize the total amount of learning you accomplish in this class, defined as lasting, conceptual understanding of the issues in biosecurity that you will be able to draw upon a year or more from now when issues arise in your work and/or in your role as a citizen in a society where biosecurity issues are increasingly salient.

**Philosophy/assumptions:** The primary goal of taking this class is to produce that kind of lasting learning, rather than to produce the “best” possible outputs as course papers. As such, success will be measured by the delta in students’ ability to use concepts they have learned in new settings and for new questions, as will inevitably arise within months for AI (Part 3), and within your lifetimes, maybe soon, for pandemics (Part 2), and throughout your lives if you become researchers in the case of ecosystem questions (Part 4).

You are among the last people in US history who will have spent part of your lives forced to struggle with concepts before turning to AI to explain them to you, because the latter was not an option until a few years ago, by which time you had at least entered if not finished high school. Most of you will therefore remember that struggling to understand ideas yourself produces more durable and more complete mastery of those ideas than simply having them explained to you, by a person or a machine.

There is a clear analogy to physical training (after writing this I saw that the New Yorker [agrees](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-weekend-essay/should-the-classroom-be-more-like-the-gym)). Physical exercise, including working to the limits of our current ability, is a requirement for improving our personal stamina and ability to walk, run, lift, and do other tasks. Doing those things with only our own muscles is beneficial (and for some people even enjoyable) despite the fact that many of us will almost always use a car to get to a place 26 miles away. Similarly, struggling with ideas and how they fit together is beneficial (and for some people even enjoyable) despite the fact that many of us will also use AI to do most of our work. This makes complete sense if the goal of exertion is to build our capacities, while the goal of “working in real life” is to produce the best possible output. We could all complete marathons faster in a car, but no one thinks you should build your physical endurance by driving a car.

Finally, an underappreciated risk of AI is that the human activity of discussing ideas and finding expertise among friends and colleagues may be rapidly displaced to conversations with machines. You have for four years or so the exceptional privilege of being around exceptional people–your fellow students. It is a pleasure and an opportunity not to be missed to discuss ideas with them. Don’t let Claude substitute for these opportunities to connect with your fellow humans.

## Policy

For this class, you MAY NOT use AI for the following tasks:

1. To create first drafts of, or edit for content your two reaction papers or your final presentation. This includes that you should not use AI to generate the ideas in your papers or the connections between them. As noted below you may use AI for grammar and spelling and standard American English, but not to generate content, which includes the connections between parts of the content.
2. To suggest initial approaches to a problem you are working on for one of these papers
3. For any part of your contributions to the discussion board.

For this class, you MAY use AI for the following tasks, and you should include on each assignment a brief description of what you used it for:

1. To summarize those readings in which the syllabus specifically recommends this as an option (some readings are more detailed than is beneficial for most students)
2. As augmented search engines: where in the past you would have entered “Biosecurity and China” into Google or pubmed, you can now have a conversation with AI starting with a prompt like “What are some articles in English that assess the Chinese approach to biosecurity?” and conversational follow-ups.
3. To discuss concepts after you have given them careful thought by yourself and sought at least one human (fellow student, faculty, or other) to discuss them. This does not mean that the tool’s output should be used as a first draft but that it can help you to understand concepts as a discussion with another person would. If the concept is suggested to you by Claude, you should cite it in your work as you would an idea from a human.
4. To help write code or generate figures for your final project – because these activities are not the focus of the class. By generate figures, I mean you are telling it what you want to express, and asking for a graphic representation, not asking it to figure out what you want to express. For example: “draw a figure with time on the x-axis and incidence of an infection on the y-axis that follows approximately SIR dynamics and mark the peak as the point where R_effective=1” NOT “draw a figure of how an epidemic looks”
5. To generate slides for your oral presentation, with the same stipulation as the previous point – you need to tell it in detail what is on the slides.
6. To spell-check and grammar-check.

As a rule of thumb, if using AI for a task replaces productive struggle with concepts or tradeoffs or how to compare lost education with reduced disease incidence or what are the chokepoints for preventing misuse of biology, don’t use it. If it replaces low-value tedium like searching for articles or drawing a nice graph, use it.

## Incentives

I will try to align incentives to the goals stated above. For example:

1. Discussion board participation, part of a significant part of your grade that is for participation, will be primarily graded for completeness, with some extra for those whose comments that engage most fully with, and stimulate, the other students’ comments. Ideas that are more interesting than average will likely not come from AI.
2. The final presentation will be oral as well as written, with the rule that you may not read text from slides. In fact, slides will be optional for the oral presentation.
3. I will talk to you about your written work.
4. The reaction papers will be graded with an eye toward seeing the specific view you bring to the topic as it relates to your own experience.
5. I will ask you to write papers from the start as Google docs and to turn them in by sharing with me. This will allow me to see your revision history. I don’t have time or interest to pore over this, but it provides a common body of data if an issue arises.

## Parting thoughts

I understand that this policy is only partially enforceable and that it can be gamed. Your experience and learning are so much more valuable than a fractional increase in grade that I sincerely believe that adhering to the spirit of the policy is in your best interest. I am also not naive enough to think that this policy is 100% enforceable by me, but I’m relying on your self-interest and the Stanford Honor Code to make this policy work.

I reserve the right to clarify or extend this policy in the course of the quarter, as this is my first teaching experience at Stanford, and indeed also because AI capabilities may change during the quarter! I welcome feedback about the policy, and am happy to discuss any questions you may have.

I have used Claude to create slides, consistent with my encouraging you to do so, since I don’t think slide creation is much of a learning experience. I have in one or two cases (noted in the materials) also used Claude to do substantive work in creating course materials, which I then checked for correctness. This is because my goal as stated is your learning, not mine, and I see little difference between using a paper by another scholar as a learning material and using a piece of text generated by Claude, from that perspective.
