> **AI policy:** **TL;DR** The policy aims to maximize your learning. You may use AI for things you could ordinarily use a friend or classmate for (discussion, for example), **but** try having those discussions with people first. You may also use it for defined tasks that aren't central to the course's learning goals, such as drawing figures and making slides.

## You may NOT use AI to

1. **Create first drafts of, or edit for content,** your two reaction papers or your final presentation. This includes generating the ideas in your papers or the connections between them. (Grammar, spelling, and standard American English are fine.)
2. **Suggest initial approaches** to a problem you are working on for one of these papers.
3. Write **any part of your discussion board contributions**.

## You MAY use AI to

Include a brief note on each assignment describing what you used it for.

1. **Summarize readings** where the syllabus specifically says this is an option.
2. **Search for sources**, e.g. "What are some articles in English that assess the Chinese approach to biosecurity?" with conversational follow-ups.
3. **Discuss concepts** after you have thought them through yourself *and* discussed them with at least one human. Its output is not a first draft. If an idea comes from the AI, cite it as you would an idea from a person.
4. **Write code or generate figures** for your final project, when you specify what to express. Good: "draw a figure with time on the x-axis and incidence on the y-axis following approximately SIR dynamics, and mark the peak where R_effective = 1." Not: "draw a figure of how an epidemic looks."
5. **Generate slides** for your oral presentation, with the same condition: you say in detail what goes on them.
6. **Spell-check and grammar-check.**

> **Tip:** **Rule of thumb:** if AI would replace productive struggle with concepts or tradeoffs (how to weigh lost education against reduced disease incidence, where the chokepoints for preventing misuse of biology are), don't use it. If it would replace low-value tedium like searching for articles or drawing a nice graph, go ahead.

## Why

The goal is lasting, conceptual understanding of biosecurity that you can draw on a year or more from now, in your work and as a citizen. Struggling with ideas yourself produces more durable mastery than having them explained to you, whether by a person or a machine. The syllabus compares it to physical training: no one builds endurance by driving a marathon in a car.

There is also a social reason. You have a few years surrounded by exceptional fellow students. Don't let AI substitute for discussing ideas with them.

## How incentives are aligned

1. Discussion posts are graded mostly for completeness, with extra credit for comments that engage with and stimulate other students' comments.
2. The final presentation is oral; you may not read text from slides, and slides are optional.
3. Professor Lipsitch will talk with you about your written work.
4. Reaction papers are graded for the specific view you bring from your own experience.
5. Write papers from the start as **Google Docs** and submit by sharing them with [lipsitch@stanford.edu](mailto:lipsitch@stanford.edu), so revision history is available if a question arises.

## Final notes

The policy is only partly enforceable and relies on your self-interest and the Stanford Honor Code. Professor Lipsitch may clarify or extend it during the quarter as AI capabilities change, and welcomes feedback. He has used Claude to make slides, and in one or two noted cases to create course materials that he then checked for correctness.
