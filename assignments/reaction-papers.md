## The assignment

Two short essays giving **your own response** to a reading of your choice.

Select one, or at most two, readings that were **not assigned to you**. These can be:

- readings assigned to *others* on divided-reading days,
- unassigned parts of readings we did assign,
- any of the [suggested readings](suggested-readings.md), or
- something not in the syllabus at all, including a video, podcast, or recent news item.

> **Important:** **This is not a summary.** Engage with the reading by taking issue with it, filling out its argument, or using it as a jumping-off point for an idea of your own. You may summarize the part you're debating, filling out, or jumping off from (though not jumping completely off: engagement with the material is key), but summarizing isn't the goal. **Cite the reading.**

## What Professor Lipsitch is looking for

- **Disagreeing is fine.** Arguing against a position he stated, or one in an assigned reading, costs you nothing. Many favorite papers made him reconsider something.
- **Make personal experience do argumentative work.** Draw on your background, community, or research, but find the general lesson that follows from the particular experience rather than letting the experience speak for itself.
- **Connect to the course.** Bring any source, however far outside the syllabus, into contact with our readings, lectures, or recurring themes.
- **Represent the reading accurately.** Misstating what an author argues limits the grade however good the rest of the essay is.
- **Go one step further than feels necessary.** The most common shortfall is a good idea stated and dropped. Work out implications, name obstacles and how they might be addressed, and say what would have to be true for your argument to hold.

> **Note:** **Length:** 500–1000 words is a guide, not a limit. Going over isn't penalized but earns nothing by itself. Most students spend about **1.5–2 hours** of active writing/editing per paper.

## Grading

The two **key criteria** are your own contribution and accurate, substantive engagement with the reading. Without them the paper won't get a good grade. Weights are approximate, conveying relative emphasis.

| Criterion | Weight | What earns credit |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Your own contribution** (key) | ~35% | Goes somewhere the reading does not: takes issue with it, supplies a missing piece, or uses it as a launch point. A specific experience or case becomes a transferable lesson. |
| **Engagement with the reading** (key) | ~20% | The argument is represented as the author actually made it, with specific passages, figures, or mechanisms cited rather than gestured at. |
| Argumentative rigor | ~20% | Claims are supported, not asserted. Counterarguments raised and answered. Comparative claims (one risk larger than another) are actually argued. Internally consistent. |
| Connection to the course | ~15% | Draws on class readings, lectures, or recurring themes. |
| Development | ~10% | The idea is carried through to its implications, obstacles, and conditions. |

A paper that misreads or never really engages its source won't reach the A range on the strength of the other criteria. Nor will an accurate, thorough paper that adds nothing of the author's own.

> A paper is never marked down for reaching a conclusion I disagree with. It is marked down for being easy to agree with because it has not said anything of its own.
